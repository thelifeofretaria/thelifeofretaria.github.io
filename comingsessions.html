Session 15

November 18 at 15h00 (CET)

This session will be about Indian and Southern Ocean Ecology, with:

Qingxia Li
Distinct responses of abundant and rare foraminifera to environmental variables in the Antarctic region revealed by DNA metabarcoding
Microbial communities are composed of a few abundant species and a large number of rare species. Microbes in the rare biosphere display specific and sometimes unique ecology and biogeography that can differ from that of abundant species. Foraminifera, a group of shell-bearing protists, are an important component of marine ecosystems and a crucial indicator for reconstructing ancient environments. However, previous molecular ecological studies on foraminifera have mostly focused on the whole community and rarely compared the ecological properties of abundant and rare taxa. Do abundant and rare species exhibit different biogeographical patterns? Do they respond consistently to environmental changes? In this study, we obtained surface sediment samples from the Southern Ocean at water depths ranging from 50 to 4399 m and measured eight environmental variables. We generated a DNA metabarcoding dataset of foraminifera and evaluated the relationships between environmental factors and the diversity of both abundant and rare foraminiferal taxa in the Antarctic region

Vaishnavi Dixit
Elucidating Benthic Foraminiferal Diversity Along the Indian Margin: Integrated Morphological and Molecular Insights
Foraminifera are a single-celled, species-rich phylum of marine rhizarian protists with a half-billion-year-old fossil record, making them indispensable for (paleo)environmental reconstruction and biomonitoring. Yet across the diverse Indian coastline bounded by the upwelling-driven, high-salinity Arabian Sea to the west and the river-fed, freshwater-stratified Bay of Bengal to the east, these protists have traditionally been classified solely on morphological characteristics, which has limitations. Our comprehensive synthesis documented 1,620 taxonomic records of foraminifera across the Indian margin; however, observed exclusive reliance on test morphology has masked regional endemism and overlooked fragile, soft-shelled lineages. Our group has been working to bridge this gap through integrative morpho-taxonomic efforts by combining test ultrastructure, morphometrics, and single-cell DNA barcoding. These surveys have revealed extensive cryptic diversity alongside vast uncharted blanks in existing genetic reference databases. Through this work, we address key questions: Is there endemism in benthic foraminifera? Can integrating morphology-based taxonomy with molecular systematics finally untangle ecophenotypes, cryptic species, polymorphic taxa, and invasive lineages? Will this approach enrich and curate regional reference databases? Ultimately, will our efforts to enrich foraminiferal DNA libraries from India unlock reliable environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding-based biotic ecological assessments?
